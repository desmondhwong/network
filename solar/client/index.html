Ephemerides

Calculated mode uses Astronomy Engine 2.1.19, a tested VSOP/lunar implementation with a stated target accuracy of about one arcminute. It supports continuous playback. The source, corrections and frames are recorded with the app.

Load JPL requests NASA/JPL Horizons data for the selected instant and observer. The request sends that UTC time, latitude, longitude and elevation to JPL. Queries are sequential and may take up to 90 seconds. Returned values replace the planet vectors, tropical longitudes and surface sky together. Playback is paused while those exact-instant data are active. A date/location change returns to clearly labelled calculated data until JPL is loaded again.

Vectors are heliocentric J2000 ecliptic coordinates in AU. Tropical positions are geocentric ecliptic longitudes of date. The surface sky uses topocentric apparent directions, with refraction excluded. Distant bodies retain selectable symbols; nearby globes and Earth-sky Sun/Moon disks use physical radii. The body radius multiplier supports values up to 1,000,000,000,000. It changes displayed sizes while orbital positions and flight reference radii stay fixed; enlarged bodies can overlap. Cached geometric positions and their paths share the bounded JPL package. Apparent sky coordinates, eclipse search and phase calculations retain their stated local model. Exact-instant JPL retrieval remains a separate paused mode. Earth-orientation predictions, future leap seconds, ΔT, atmosphere and lunar topography limit absolute precision. Extra displayed digits do not establish measurement accuracy.

Earth’s Moon

The Moon panel provides navigation, phase readouts, independent orbit styling, NASA imagery and optional LOLA terrain. Local mode draws a closed instantaneous orbital guide; cached trajectories remain open at coverage boundaries. Pin altitude is measured from the reference sphere, including when visible terrain is displaced. Terrain does not add collision or terrain-following motion. Near the ground, rendering falls back to the reference sphere. Lunar maps: NASA SVS CGI Moon Kit, Ernie Wright and the LRO camera/laser-altimeter teams.

ISS data and observation

Automatic source selection uses a covering NASA OEM trajectory, then usable GP/SGP4, then an explicitly illustrative circle. Source epoch, download time, coverage and provider comparisons appear in the ISS panel. The optional local server refreshes no more often than every two hours; failures retain good data and pause automatic retries. Saved bundles work offline. Observer tools show azimuth/elevation, a spherical horizon footprint and next-day passes. Visibility assumes sunlight at the station and Sun below −6° at the observer; terrain, weather and refraction are excluded. The station model uses sourced dimensions in an approximate assembly, with six 2023 iROSA additions and illustrative attitude. Its onboard camera follows the predicted orbit. Opt-in telemetry displays timestamped candidate fields but does not drive geometry while its frame conventions remain unvalidated.

Flight and tether

One perspective camera travels through one 3D scene. L toggles orbital following without changing the camera position, orientation or field of view. On natural bodies, F toggles surface spin lock at the current pose. Hover a body to highlight a target, or choose it from Destination. Unavailable targets fall back to the nearest supported body. Shift+L frames and follows the destination; Shift+F lands upright on its reference sphere. F enables orbital following with surface spin lock; releasing F keeps orbital following, while releasing L returns to inertial flight. Destination jumps and Shift+L / Shift+F transitions animate when Animate navigation is on; switch it off for immediate moves. An explicit On choice enables animation even when the operating system prefers reduced motion. Double-click a body in the list to jump. The first number-key jump preserves the previous body-relative view; repeat the same key to cycle the default, solar-plane and surface views. Release returns to inertial free flight without moving the camera.

On natural bodies, surface lock and Pin mode are deliberate separate choices. F adds spin following with free movement in the camera frame. G enters or leaves Pin mode at the current position and view, dropping its footprint onto the closest point of the target sphere. The current facing direction becomes the pin’s forward heading. Shift+G enters Pin mode, resets both pitch and roll to the local tangent and animates down to 2 m when animation is enabled. Landing upright or approaching a body does not enter Pin mode. In Pin mode W A S D and Shift-drag move parallel to the local tangent; Q / E changes radial height. Speed scales with the body radius, starts at 10×, and can be adjusted with the Pin speed multiplier. Looking, orbit, roll and pitch change the pinned view without changing altitude. Manual pitch and roll remain relative to the local surface as you travel; R resets them to zero. In free flight, attitude is measured against the Sun’s Galactic orbital plane and R resets to that plane; WASDQE follows the camera’s viewing frame. Shift slows movement. Left / right arrows roll and up / down arrows pitch. Hold comma / period to yaw the actual camera left / right, so free flight follows the turned view. Releasing the key stops turning and centers the input dial while retaining the new heading. R clears held yaw input as well as resetting attitude. Mouse look captures the pointer after a click; Escape releases it. Flight reference surfaces are spheres. Optional lunar terrain is visual; collision and terrain-following motion are not modeled.

On ISS, F / G and landing use its fixed onboard view; initial boarding moves the camera there. Translation stays fixed while onboard. Leave onboard releases it.

The complete Sun orbit is a separate optional guide around Sagittarius A*. Sun and planet trails share the selected elapsed interval, from three months to 100,000 years. They use the same UTC-driven circular Galactic model (8.2 kpc, 230 km/s) as the moving Sun. Distant trails simplify to centerlines; approaching adds bounded local orbital detail. Long spans are illustrative trajectories, not predictions across geological time. Galactic-rate playback advances the same date as ordinary playback.

Every supported Sun, planet and Moon has a mini globe. Outside Pin mode it stays a stationary preview without a pin; double-click a surface point to enter Pin mode there. The scene pin is also visible only in Pin mode. Surface opacity, rotating markings, texture and coordinate grid are independent layers. Earth offers a bundled NASA Blue Marble Next Generation satellite-derived July 2004 composite, with satellite, pixel and dot presentation and a display-detail control. The image stays fixed as simulation time changes; display detail does not improve the source resolution. Schematic land/ocean colors and the retained line map remain independent options, with no terrain elevation or live imagery. Grid spacing and opacity apply to the scene and mini globe. In Pin mode the pin visualizes the surface camera and can be hidden. Its base shares exact body-fixed coordinates with the camera footprint and its head follows camera height, including Q / E movement. The mini-globe stem follows the body’s surface normal. In the main view, the stem is a visible camera-anchored cue from the lower edge to the projected ground point, with independent stem and point color, size and opacity. Drag the mini-globe ball upward to raise altitude and downward to lower it; drag the base to move the footprint. Near-ground height is visibly magnified for the handle and labeled. At high altitude the mini globe scales the sphere and stem together. Earth additionally has its geographic line map, city labels, GPS coordinates and numerical sky. Rotating markings make non-Earth spin visible and can be switched off. The Sun and gas giants have nominal reference spheres, not solid landing surfaces. No device geolocation is read.

Show non-Earth moons hides the moons of other planets while preserving Earth’s Moon. Show all moons and each moon’s individual visibility choice still apply. ISS has its own visibility control in the ISS panel.

Remote dates

The calendar accepts any signed decimal year, including year 0 (1 BCE), with exact arithmetic. Dates beyond the analytical engine envelope use an explicitly illustrative periodic model, not an ephemeris. Analytical dates outside 1800–2200 carry no validated accuracy guarantee. A finite calculation is not proof of historical or predictive precision. JPL retrieval and eclipse search retain the verified 1800–2200 interval. The source guide records the range probes and model limitations.

Stars and constellations

Expanded XHIP bright and nearby stars use quality-filtered parallax distances and measured proper motions. Accepted radial velocities are included where available; otherwise that component is assumed zero. Other distances are explicitly illustrative. Constellation lines and optional names use the same spatial stars and distort away from the Solar neighborhood. Stellar motion can be switched off. Long-time Galactic paths use a bounded analytic model, not a gravitationally integrated or predictive Milky Way simulation. Binary motion, stellar evolution and lifetimes are omitted. The catalogue-kinematics label within ±1,000 Julian years of J2000 is a presentation boundary, not a verified accuracy range; more remote motion is labelled illustrative.

Milky Way context

The optional Milky Way overview combines bounded schematic 3D arms, disk, bar/bulge and stellar halo with 2,024 observed cluster centers: 1,867 Gaia DR2-based open clusters from Cantat-Gaudin 2020 and 157 globular clusters from Harris 1996/2010. Clouds around selected centers illustrate members using the source projected half radii; they are not measured 3D member positions. The retained measured nearby-star layer is separate. Open-cluster coordinates use epoch 2015.5; globular coordinates use equinox J2000. The pattern and centers remain fixed in inertial Galactic axes while the modeled Sun moves. Cluster motion and Galactic evolution are not integrated; remote epochs remain illustrative. Opacity, bounded detail and cluster annotations are adjustable. Cluster names and marker circles share one toggle; bright center dots and stellar clouds remain visible without annotations.

Pluto uses Astronomy Engine’s integrated model near years 1–3998. Outside that interval its orbit is a circular illustration, labeled in the body readout.

Astrology and eclipses

The dedicated Astrology section is available in Earth context. It combines live signs and longitudes for the Sun, Moon and all other planets, ASC / DSC, MC / IC, sidereal time and alignments with the zodiac display. Show astrology overlays hides every astrology drawing and label together while retaining individual choices and numerical calculations. Earth has no direction from its own center, so it is omitted from the geocentric point controls. Outlines divide the ecliptic into twelve 30° sectors; colors, radii and opacity are adjustable. Filled wedges are removed. Independent longitude grid spacing accepts any value from 1° to 90°, with separate color, opacity and width. Marked sliders snap to common values, and adjacent number fields allow exact radii and opacity. Three global switches control ring point markers, labels and ecliptic longitudes. Longitude lines start off; their subordinate planet-point switch adds markers at the bodies’ actual ecliptic latitude when lines are on. Ring labels retain a separate placement for every object. Ring points are projected onto the ecliptic at their longitude and share the ring radius with their guides. Each object uses a consistent color for its marker, label and guide. Translucent Earth lets these celestial overlays show through; far-side surface markings stay hidden. Hover a point or grid coordinate for its model coordinates. Markers lie on an adjustable Earth-centered diagram, not at the physical body distances. These geometric relationships carry no predictive interpretation.

The dedicated Eclipses section searches solar or lunar events. Viewing an eclipse places the camera on Earth and aims toward the event sky; normal surface controls remain available. Local horizon, atmosphere and lunar limb details limit real-world visibility and contact precision.

Controls

On touch screens, drag with one finger to look; use two fingers to pan and pinch to travel. Panel opens the same controls on a small screen. Touch flight in Flight and destination provides held movement, roll and pitch, fine movement, attitude reset and field of view. The body globe supports the same surface and pin handles with touch.

Sidebar sections start closed on refresh. Only the top level of each sidebar section folds; all its controls remain together. With a section summary focused, Up / Down moves between visible sections, Left closes, Right opens, and Home / End selects the first / last section. Form controls retain their own arrow keys. The whole sidebar scrolls to its final control above the timeline. The viewing-area keyboard focus border fades shortly after activation. H hides all panels or shows them all when any panel is hidden; B hides the top bar, P the sidebar and T the complete timeline. Edge buttons restore them without taking space from the field. O opens Workspace. The heading identifies the selected body, orbit lock, spin lock and Pin mode. Optional numerical flight rows show position, flight speed, pin speed, relative body motion, scale and roll/pitch attitude. Pin mode adds a compass heading. N is 0°, E 90°, S 180° and W 270° in the stated reference frame. Separate attitude and yaw instruments share a dedicated Flight director section. Their compressed pitch diagram retains a visible horizon at steep angles; numerical angles carry the exact values. Escape clears selection after closing any dialog or releasing mouse look; clicking the selected body clears it too. Automatic timeline span follows playback rate; a manual span turns automatic scaling off. The timeline holds the UTC, display-zone and Pin clocks, with editable date and exact time together. Time options can align ruler ticks and snapping with the display time zone. Repeated local hours include their UTC offsets; dates outside civil-time support use a labelled UTC ruler. Now / N returns to the current UTC instant and preserves playback. Live now follows wall-clock time at 1×, including after sleep. Editing time or speed leaves Live mode. ⌘K / Ctrl+K opens searchable commands; Find reveals a setting in its panel.
